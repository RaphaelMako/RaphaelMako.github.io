The other day I read an article in The Economist that particularly affected me: _Britons are less bored than they used to be. This is bad_[^1]. It took me by surprise because I couldn’t remember the last time I felt that dull sense of boredom. Growing up it seemed to surround me on long car rides, or sweaty summer days. I absolutely dreaded the feeling, the slow whittling down of life, the burden of nothingness. And yet, now I missed it. It would be a gross understatement to say social media has fundamentally changed the way we interact with each other and the world; but I am most interested in how it has changed our relationship to ourselves and what this means for the future. In an age where AI is becoming more prevalent in our lives and it is able to outsource cognitive load and knowledge creation, I fear there is a real concern that we may forget how to think and learn, but this doesn’t have to be the case.

I grew up just as social media was taking off and it was still relatively unsophisticated. At 13 I had a Facebook account, but at that time it was just used to induce fomo in your friends, and post highly filtered photos of yourself. What this meant was that while social media was present in my high school years it didn’t consume me. But this isn’t the case for the teens of today. Some studies have found teenagers spending on average 4.8 hours a day on social media along with a checklist of mental health disorders and psychological imbalances[^2]. Despite the fact older generations have criticized the habits of the young throughout all of humanity (before social media it was video games, before video games it was cassettes, I’m sure all the way back to those darn teens never peeling their eyes off of fire), this time it’s markedly different.

Previous fads that exploded among youth throughout history were, well, fads. They came and went. Kids would obsess over them, but eventually move on - perhaps more aptly said, the fads didn’t move along with them. However, social media is distinct from those previous fads, specifically for three major reasons. Firstly and most significantly, it is specifically designed to adjust to user preferences and the algorithms that present users content are studied and developed with teams of psychologists to ensure maximal user retention. Secondly, social media is nearly universal, 95% of teens (age 13-17) use social media daily[^3], meaning being in the out group means being in the extreme minority. Finally, as the name suggests, it is a means for socializing and communicating amongst peers. Whether by sharing Instagram Reels, or using them as primary means of messaging, for many people these apps are an essential tool for keeping in contact with friends.

The reliance on social media and its effects on people’s mental health has already been widely regarded as a crisis[^4], but there is also a subtler undercurrent that is harder to study. It has eliminated much of the friction we face on a daily basis. The thing that makes social media so appealing is the exact thing that makes it so harmful. Boredom is a cognitive roadblock, and learning how to address it and move past it is a metacognitive skill that is essential in developing executive function. By avoiding dealing with boredom through social media we are weakening this metacognitive process that is crucial in many other aspects of life. For example, learning, paying attention, planning and emotional regulation are all instances of where we face cognitive friction, but by addressing it we become more capable at addressing it in the future.

In cognitive science there is a concept that says “neurons that fire together wire together”. This saying is the basis of habit forming in the brain. It refers to the brain’s ability to learn and quite literally rewire itself to optimise for relevant tasks. If we wake up everyday and go to the gym to workout, we’ll build a physical connection in the brain that associates working out to waking up and this ensures that we continue performing these positive habits. However, this can also work in the reverse. Just as we can form positive habits that enrich our lives, the same process is what results in bad habits and in the most severe situations takes the form of addictions. In the case of social media, we’ve seen it used as a near universal bandaid to fight boredom. Every time we are alone and we decide to look at our phones to distract ourselves, we encourage our brains’ neurons to fire and then wire together. This encourages a behaviour that abhors instances of minimal or unpleasant stimulation/friction and consequently weakens our ability to deal with this pain and discomfort.

This leads to my big concern. With the advent of AI we have a whole new wave of very capable tools. In theory, AI can open up many new opportunities and/or improve productivity; it can also put our already unstable cognitive health at risk. Doing writing tasks like essays and papers, or even simpler things like writing emails are time consuming and for the vast majority of people difficult to do. But this difficulty is exactly what our brains need to function. Just like a muscle, if we neglect using it, it will atrophy. Today we have more access than ever before to skip this practice altogether. We can simply throw a prompt at an LLM and watch as the AI spits out an essay that may have taken two weeks, in ten seconds. It’s painful to write, putting ideas on the page, when it simply does not want to find itself there. Ironically this short post has taken me about a month and a half to write, but I insisted on continuing on because I don’t want to lose the single thing I truly own - my thinking. Sitting with ideas helps us process information and investigate our own perspectives on the world. However, people of today are more primed than ever to rely on AI because of our early exposure to social media.

Our inability to push through the pain of boredom extends itself to a broader inability to moderate our impulses. Now with AI, we can once more avoid the pain of thinking by simply getting AI to do it for us. But this is a mistake. If we give into the urge to take shortcuts we leave ourselves vulnerable to forgetting how to think altogether. We will convince ourselves that a small shortcut here is not much worse than a small shortcut there, much like a small bite of a cake can end up being a large portion if we keep letting ourselves “have just a little bit more”. Worst of all, I worry how these tools will impact future generations of kids who will never not have access to them. Perhaps they will have much stronger characters than us since they will be forced to grow up in a world with everything at their disposal. However, testing to see what happens is not something I think is worth the risk. We have already seen how kids use social media and the deleterious effects it has on their psyche, now supercharge that with a tool that can allow them to stop thinking altogether, and who knows what could happen. I certainly don’t want to chance finding out.
